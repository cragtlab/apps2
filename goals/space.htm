Goal / space hardware

Build the part of a spacecraft that pays for itself.

A Singapore-first plan for a company that can sell to satellites now, earn space heritage next, and make a credible lunar variant later.

The recommendation Start with a rugged avionics + power/thermal controller for small satellites and lunar payloads.

Keep the high-value work in Singapore: requirements, electronics, firmware, autonomy, qualification evidence and customer integration. Buy launch access and specialist test capacity from partners. The first product should be a subsystem, not a rocket, lander or entire constellation.

01 / Why this can work

Singapore is a good base for the valuable middle of the stack.

The opportunity is not “put a Singapore flag on a moonshot.” It is using Singapore’s engineering, finance, logistics and regional customer access to turn a difficult subsystem into a repeatable export product.

70+

Space companies are active across Singapore’s value chain, according to NSAS.

2,000

Professionals and researchers are counted in the local space ecosystem.

S$210m

Government funding set aside since 2022 through the Space Technology Development Programme.

US$2.6b

Maximum combined value of NASA CLPS contracts through November 2028—not a guarantee of accessible revenue.

The first two signals come from NSAS’s ecosystem page; the S$210m figure is from the current STDP/NSAS pages; the last is NASA’s current CLPS contract ceiling. The practical implication is a real but competitive market for proven components.

The business thesis

Sell reliability evidence, not just hardware. A flight customer is buying a component, a qualification plan, test data, documentation, delivery confidence and someone who will answer the phone during a mission.

Use Earth and LEO customers to pay for the learning curve. Treat the Moon as a premium qualification branch, not as the only market.

  1. Find a painful interfaceTalk to satellite and payload integrators before freezing a board, connector or enclosure.
  2. Build one modular coreKeep the power, compute, telemetry and fault-management architecture common across LEO and lunar variants.
  3. Charge for the workFund engineering through paid studies and non-recurring engineering (NRE), then sell units and mission support.
  4. Earn heritage in stepsTerrestrial demo → LEO technology demonstration → lunar payload integration.
02 / The first product

ORBIT-MOON: a modular payload controller.

Working name only. The product is a flight-like controller that powers, monitors and autonomously protects a customer’s sensor, experiment, communications payload or rover subsystem.

P

Power conditioning

Accept a customer-defined spacecraft bus input, create protected rails, measure current/voltage, and shut down a failed load without taking the mission with it.

C

Edge compute

Run filtering, compression, event detection or autonomy locally so customers can downlink useful results instead of every raw byte.

I/O

Payload interface

Offer a small, documented set of robust interfaces—such as CAN-FD, RS-422 and SpaceWire where the mission requires it—rather than an everything-board.

T

Thermal + mechanical

Give the customer a defined mounting pattern, heat path, harness, mass properties and thermal model. Integration friction is part of the product.

F

Fault management

Use watchdogs, ECC memory where appropriate, brown-out handling, safe modes, command authentication and a clear fault-log format.

V

Verification pack

Ship the test procedure, raw data, calibration records, configuration baseline and known limitations with every flight unit.

Version 0 design targets

Use these only to start customer conversations: 6U/12U-compatible envelope, roughly 15–30 W average and 50 W peak, 2–4 kg total module, redundant load switching, and a firmware interface that can be reused across missions. The actual bus voltage, radiation tolerance, connector, thermal limits and interface standards must be set by a signed customer requirement.

Buyers pay forLower integration time, better fault isolation, a faster payload path and evidence that survives design review.
Do not promise yet“Moon qualified” or “radiation proof.” Publish the mission envelope and the tests actually completed.
Commercial moatReference designs plus flight data, test automation, component knowledge and customer-specific adapters.
Build / test routes

There are four sensible ways to get to space.

Choose the cheapest route that creates evidence a customer can buy. You do not need to own a launch vehicle, a satellite constellation or a lunar lander to become a space company.

Route A / space-enabled now

Sell the data outcome first.

Build an Earth-observation, maritime or logistics product using existing satellite data and a terrestrial or airborne pilot. This proves the workflow and customer value before you manufacture flight hardware.

Proof: paid pilot, repeatable data pipeline, measurable decision or cost improvement.
Route B / component supplier

Build a subsystem for an integrator.

Make the controller, power stage, payload interface or software tool. Use a customer-owned mission and partner labs for specialist environmental testing; keep your reusable IP and verification pack.

Proof: signed ICD, engineering model, test report, customer NRE or purchase order.
Route C / technology demo

Fly a hosted payload or rideshare.

After the bench and environmental evidence, take a small experiment through NSAS’s Space Access Programme or a university/industry payload partner. Treat the first flight as a controlled technology demonstration.

Proof: accepted payload interface, mission funding, operations rehearsal and usable flight data.
Route D / lunar later

Join a lunar payload team.

Adapt a LEO-proven core for a named lander, rover or payload integrator. NASA CLPS is a useful demand signal, but its current provider pool is American companies; a Singapore supplier should enter through partners.

Proof: lunar ICD, funded qualification plan and a customer who owns the mission opportunity.
Verification ladder

Test the same failure modes at increasing fidelity.

Write the requirements and verification matrix before buying expensive test time. Tailor the levels to the mission and customer ICD.

01

Model

Requirements, interfaces, FMEA, power/thermal model, software unit tests and software-in-the-loop fault cases.

02

Bench

Engineering model, payload emulator, programmable supply, current/temperature logging, command loss and brown-out injection.

03

Representative

Flight-like harness and enclosure, realistic mounting, thermal margins, long-duration soak, EMI pre-scan and repeatability run.

04

Environmental

Partner-lab thermal-vacuum/bakeout, vibration or shock, EMI/EMC and functional retest, with calibrated data and witnessed configuration.

05

Flight

Acceptance test, end-to-end operations rehearsal, launch-site procedures, telemetry monitoring, anomaly response and post-flight report.

Build/test rule: make one test article sacrificial for abuse and keep one clean article for the next step. Test “as you fly” when possible, but do not call a bench demo flight-qualified; NASA’s active GEVS baseline is a useful reference for tailoring environmental verification.
03 / The operating roadmap

The first 12 weeks decide whether the next 12 months are worth funding.

This is the working sequence: customer evidence first, a useful demo second, a paid commitment third. Hardware work begins only after the problem and interface are specific enough to fund.

Near-term objective: become customer-backed by day 90.

By the end of month three, you are not trying to have a flight board. You are trying to have one signed requirements baseline, one paid study, a demo that exposes the integration pain, and a credible quote for the next phase.

The first 12 weeks / actions and outputs
Month 01 / discover

Choose the painful interface.

  • W1Assign one owner for customer discovery and one for systems engineering. Build a list of 20 satellite, payload and Earth-observation accounts. Write a 30-minute interview script.
  • W2Complete five interviews. Ask what failed, what was delayed, which interface is bespoke, and what evidence is needed at the next design review. Do not pitch a finished board.
  • W3Complete five more interviews and two technical reviews. Sketch three wedges: power protection, edge compute, or payload I/O. Record the buyer, mission, budget owner and deadline for each.
  • W4Score the wedges on pain, repeatability, testability in Singapore, export complexity and time to paid pilot. Select one customer problem and one fallback dual-use market.
Deliverables20-account list · interview notes · wedge scorecard · one-page problem brief · named design partner
Month 02 / prove value

Make the smallest useful demo.

  • W5Turn the problem brief into a one-page offer: customer input, failure mode, demo promise, timeline and price. Ask the design partner to sign a paid feasibility study.
  • W6Assemble a bench kit from development hardware: payload emulator, protected power path, thermal sensors, fault injection and a simple operations dashboard. Label what is not flight hardware.
  • W7Run the demo with the customer. Inject brown-outs, bad telemetry, sensor faults and command loss. Measure recovery time, useful data returned and engineering hours saved.
  • W8Run a second demo or repeatability test. Convert the findings into a draft interface-control document, risk register and NRE quote. Contact an environmental-test partner for budgetary pricing.
Deliverablesworking evaluation kit · recorded failure logs · draft ICD · risk register · paid-study proposal
Month 03 / commit

Earn the right to build the EM.

  • W9Freeze the v0 product boundary: supported bus input, interfaces, mass/power envelope, software API, test evidence and explicit exclusions. Get supplier quotes for the long-lead parts.
  • W10Review the requirements with the design partner. Ask for a letter of intent or paid NRE, a target mission window and a named technical approver. Start an export-control classification review.
  • W11Build the 6–9 month plan: engineering model, test sequence, staffing, cash milestones, external labs and space-access route. Discuss the project with NSAS/STDP using the customer case.
  • W12Hold the investment gate. Sign the requirements baseline and paid study, or pivot the wedge. Only then release the engineering-model BOM and begin detailed PCB/mechanical design.
Deliverablessigned requirements baseline · NRE quote · supplier plan · test budget · grant/partner brief · go/pivot decision
Day-90 gateGo only if a real customer has accepted the problem, paid or committed to the study, and agreed to the interface review. If not, keep the demo assets and pivot before adding flight-grade cost.

Keep the brain local

  • Systems engineering, requirements, architecture and configuration control
  • PCB, firmware, FPGA/compute, autonomy and test automation
  • Mechanical/thermal design, harness drawings and integration
  • Customer support, IP ownership, documentation and export classification
  • Regional sales to satellite, maritime, aviation and geospatial customers

Rent the expensive edge cases

  • Launch, rideshare, lunar lander and payload-integration services
  • Radiation testing, thermal-vacuum, vibration and specialist EMC capacity until volume justifies ownership
  • Flight-rated or rad-tolerant parts with long-lead and second-source planning
  • High-reliability assembly and environmental test witnessing when customers require it
  • Mission insurance and legal advice for spacecraft ownership or operation
04 / Make it profitable

Charge first for engineering. Scale later on units and software.

The cash-flow trap is funding qualification for free and hoping unit volume appears. Every custom request should either become reusable IP or be paid for by the customer.

Layer 01 / NRE

Mission study + adaptation

Requirements, interface definition, architecture, analysis, prototype and qualification plan.

Planning range: S$50k–S$800k

Use a small paid study to qualify the buyer before accepting a larger NRE contract.

Layer 02 / hardware

Engineering + flight units

Sell the module, adapters, spares, acceptance test and documentation. Keep the core standard and price the interface changes separately.

Planning range: S$180k–S$350k / unit

These are internal planning ranges, not quoted market prices.

Layer 03 / mission support

Health data + support

Configuration control, firmware updates, ground-tool licences, mission analysis and fleet health reporting.

Planning range: S$24k–S$60k / year

Support and software can become the highest-margin layer after the first flight.

Illustrative first pilotS$500k NRE + two units at S$240k each = S$980k contract value.
Illustrative delivery mathIf hardware direct cost is 45% of unit revenue and NRE delivery cost is S$280k, contribution before company overhead is about S$484k. Replace every assumption with supplier quotes and payroll before raising money.
Healthy sales shapeThree design partners, at least one paid flight opportunity, and a path to six or more repeat units a year before adding a large factory or lab.
Cash ruleMilestone-bill NRE; take a deposit for long-lead components; keep customer-owned mission customisation out of the standard product cost.
Profit test: if a customer will not pay for a requirements study, will not name the flight opportunity, or cannot explain who owns the integration risk, do not finance the project with your own balance sheet.
05 / Find the buyers

Sell to the integrator who is already late.

Do not begin with a broad “space market” slide. Pick one payload class and one integration pain. Your first customer should be able to tell you exactly what review, test or launch milestone your product helps them pass.

The strongest wedge is a dual-use controller: it helps satellite customers in LEO and can later be adapted for lunar payloads.

Small-satellite OEM / mission integratorNeeds a repeatable payload interface, power protection and less bespoke firmware. Offer an evaluation kit and a paid interface study.
Lunar lander or payload supplierNeeds a low-mass controller with evidence, telemetry and fault recovery. Offer the LEO-proven core plus a mission-specific lunar qualification plan.
Earth-observation / maritime operatorNeeds more useful data per pass and lower ground-processing cost. Offer edge detection/compression with a terrestrial or airborne pilot before flight.
Research institute / university payload teamNeeds an accessible flight path and engineering support. Offer a standard development kit, paid integration and a path into the NSAS Space Access Programme.
06 / Use the Singapore stack

Map each milestone to the right programme.

Singapore’s current space support is structured around capability development, validation and space access. Apply with a customer and a delivery plan, not with a vague moon ambition.

STDP · Technology Development

For a technology that addresses a business or national need and has a clear path to delivery. A customer-backed controller is a better fit than open-ended research.

STDP · Validation & Experimentation

For a shorter feasibility, rapid-prototyping or concept-validation project. Use this to test the payload interface and prove a buyer’s operational value.

STDP · Space Access Programme

For testing technology in space to achieve space heritage. The official examples include advanced materials, solar cells, edge computing and payload testing.

Latest partnership signal: NSAS–JAXA MOC, 6 July 2026.

The cooperation memorandum is intended to deepen peaceful space collaboration and create a hub for research, development and economic cooperation involving companies and universities. Treat it as a partnership door to explore, not as an automatic flight slot.

Before a flight

Contact NSAS early. The public May 2024 guideline requests registration of applicable space objects at least 30 days before planned launch, including for Singapore entities that develop, launch, own or operate a space object. Its form still carries OSTIn language, so confirm the current NSAS process in writing.

If you operate a satellite network

IMDA says a licence for use of a satellite orbital slot is needed if you plan to launch and operate a satellite network requiring an orbital slot. A component supplier may not need that licence, but the customer’s ownership and operating model still matters.

Export and technical data

Classify the hardware, firmware and technical data before sending them overseas. Singapore Customs controls strategic goods and related technology, including intangible transfer; individual permits are generally needed before controlled shipments and ITT permits before controlled technology transmission.

Quality and liability

Build a quality system, configuration control and supplier traceability early. Space guidelines point to ISO 14620, ISO 24113 and international debris/sustainability practices; get legal advice on liability, launch contracts, insurance and customer country rules.

This is a commercial planning page, not legal, export-control, grant or engineering certification advice. Ask NSAS, IMDA, Singapore Customs and a qualified lawyer or mission assurance lead for a product-specific determination.

07 / The Moon branch

Moon is a product variant, not a starting slogan.

Lunar missions are attractive because they create premium demand for mass, power and reliability. They are also unforgiving. NASA’s current CLPS page lists 13 eligible American companies and 17 lunar deliveries to five vendors, so a Singapore supplier should plan to enter through a payload or integration partner.

Go lunar when you have:

  • A named lander, rover or payload integrator and a real interface document.
  • LEO or equivalent flight evidence for the common electronics and fault-management core.
  • A funded test plan covering thermal-vacuum, radiation, vibration/shock, contamination and lunar communications/power assumptions.
  • A customer willing to pay for the lunar adaptation and accept a clear qualification boundary.

Do not start by building:

  • A launch vehicle, lunar lander or full rover.
  • A one-off science instrument with no repeatable bus interface.
  • A custom cleanroom or radiation lab before utilisation is contracted.
  • A claim that a commercial part is lunar-ready because it worked on a benchtop.
08 / The 24-month scoreboard

Measure paid evidence, not excitement.

Every phase ends with something a customer, grant reviewer or mission integrator can inspect.

0–3 months

Customer proof

  • 20 interviews
  • 2 paid studies
  • 1 signed requirements baseline
4–9 months

Engineering proof

  • Working demo
  • Pre-compliance test data
  • Flight or pilot LOI
10–15 months

Market proof

  • EM delivered
  • 1 paid flight opportunity
  • Second customer pipeline
16–24 months

Heritage proof

  • Flight data
  • Qualification report
  • Funded lunar adaptation

Kill or pivot if the evidence is missing

Stop the lunar branch if no integrator will sign an interface, no one will pay NRE, the qualification cost is larger than the reachable order book, or the product requires a different custom architecture for every customer. Pivot the same compute/power/fault-management core to Earth-observation, maritime, aviation or industrial autonomy.
